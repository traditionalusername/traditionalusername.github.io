Tim-Burners Lee, the creator of the World Wide Web, is best poised to answer the question, "what is the world wide web?” Depending on when you asked Tim, you’d likely receive different answers because as of 2026, the web is experiencing its third iteration termed the “Web 3.0.” Tim’s answer in 1991 would shift from his original intended use, to considering the subsequent social-political-technological effects. Contextual history of the world wide web, while important, burdens the scope for answering “what is the world wide web.” Truncating the question of “what is” to focus on “mechanisms,” helps define the latitude of the answer, while reflecting the logical historical development that followed these mechanisms.

The world wide web is an information space that relies on hypertext markup language (HTML) to convey information between clients and servers—across the network infrastructure of the internet. More crudely, the world wide web is an interlinked documentation system of files (webpages) that relies on browsers to interface the world wide web, and the internet as pathways to each linked document. The webpages can be thematically customized with Cascading Style Sheets (CSS) and can be furthered with interactive features with Javascript. Mr. Lee’s proposition of .html allowed these other mechanisms to functionally characterize the experience of the world wide web. Combined, these three file types sit at the tip of the iceberg for answering “what is the world wide web.”
